선배 seonbae

선배 is the most useful title on this list for a newcomer, because it is the rare Korean address term that does not depend on gender and does not depend on age. It depends on one thing: who arrived first.

Hangul선배
Revised Romanizationseonbae
McCune–Reischauersŏnbae
Literallysomeone who went before
Said byany speaker
Said toanyone who joined your school, club or company before you

What it actually means

The characters behind 선배 mean roughly "the ones before", and that is exactly the criterion. Anyone who entered your university, department, club, dojo or company ahead of you is your 선배, and you are their 후배 (hubae). A student who took a year out and enrolled at 21 is still the 선배 of a classmate who enrolled at 19 the following year. Age has nothing to do with it.

The relationship is also bounded. Someone is your 선배 with respect to a shared institution, and the title fades once you are outside it — a university 선배 you meet ten years later at an unrelated company is not your 선배 there.

선배 or 선배님

Attaching -님 raises the formality: 선배님 is what you use for a senior you do not know well, for a much older senior, and in written messages. Plain 선배 is what you settle into once you are on comfortable terms, and many seniors will explicitly tell you to drop the -님.

When they do, drop it. Continuing to say 선배님 after being asked not to reads as keeping deliberate distance, which is the opposite of polite.

What obligations come with it

In Korean student and workplace culture the 선배–후배 relationship carries real content: the senior is expected to give advice, share materials, and traditionally to pay when the group eats together, while the junior is expected to be responsive and respectful. The intensity varies enormously — a medical school or a military unit is nothing like a hobby club — but the shape is recognisable everywhere.

For a foreign learner the practical upshot is simple. Identifying someone as your 선배 gives you a correct, low-risk way to address them from the first day, before you know their age, their name or their position.

The usual mistake — Assuming 선배 means "older". It means "joined earlier", and the two frequently come apart — a younger 선배 is completely normal.

Example sentences

선배, 이 자료 어디서 찾았어요?
Seonbae, i jaryo eodiseo chajasseoyo?
Sunbae, where did you find this material?
선배님, 잠시 여쭤봐도 될까요?
Seonbae-nim, jamsi yeojjwobwado doelkkayo?
Sunbae-nim, may I ask you something?
선배가 도와주셔서 끝냈어요.
Seonbae-ga dowajusyeoseo kkeunnaesseoyo.
I finished it because you helped me.
선배, 나중에 커피 한잔해요.
Seonbae, najunge keopi hanjanhaeyo.
Sunbae, let's grab a coffee sometime.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Does sunbae depend on gender?
No. It is the same word for every speaker and every listener, which is what makes it so useful early on.
Q. Can someone younger be my sunbae?
Yes, and it happens constantly — military service, gap years and repeated entrance exams all scramble the ages within a cohort.
Q. Should I say sunbae or sunbaenim?
Start with 선배님. Move to 선배 when they tell you to, or when the group around you clearly has.
