아줌마 / 아주머니 ajumma / ajumeoni

This is the entry on this list where getting it wrong costs the most. Both words denote a middle-aged woman; only one of them is safe to say to her.

Hangul아줌마 / 아주머니
Revised Romanizationajumma / ajumeoni
McCune–Reischauerajumma / ajumŏni
Literallya middle-aged woman
Said byany speaker
Said tobest avoided as direct address

Two words, two registers

아주머니 is the standard, neutral form — the one you would use in careful speech to a woman of that age group whose name you do not know. 아줌마 is its casual contraction, and along the way it has absorbed a whole stereotype: pushy, loud, elbows on the subway, permed hair, floral visor. That caricature is doing the damage, not the literal meaning.

The result is that a woman called 아줌마 hears two things at once — a statement that she is no longer young, and an association with a figure of fun. Many women in their thirties and forties react strongly to it for precisely that reason.

Why the age line moved

The threshold has drifted upward. A generation ago the word attached to married women in their thirties; today a woman of forty may well consider it wrong for her, and 언니 is what she would rather hear in a shop or a restaurant.

This is why service workers have largely abandoned it. Walk into a clothing shop in Seoul and staff will call almost any adult female customer 언니 or 고객님, and never 아줌마.

What to say instead

Get attention with 저기요. If you need a title afterwards, use 사장님 for a woman running a shop or restaurant, 선생님 for a polite generic, 고객님 if you are the one serving, and 이모님 in the specific setting of a small restaurant where 이모 has become an affectionate convention.

If you genuinely need the descriptive noun in third person, use 아주머니 rather than 아줌마. And note that men are not exempt from the pattern — 아저씨 has the same problem in a milder form.

The usual mistake — Treating 아줌마 as a neutral word because the dictionary gloss is neutral. In direct address it is frequently heard as an insult; 저기요 costs you nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Is ajumma always rude?
Not always — friends use it about themselves as a joke, and it appears neutrally in writing. As direct address to a stranger it is risky enough that it is not worth the gamble.
Q. What is the difference from ajumeoni?
아주머니 is the full, neutral form; 아줌마 is the casual contraction that carries the stereotype. If you must use one, use 아주머니.
Q. What do shop staff call customers?
고객님, or 언니 in casual settings. Almost never 아줌마.
