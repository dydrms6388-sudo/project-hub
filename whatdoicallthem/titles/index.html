Korean titles, one page each

Twelve words that do the work English does with names and pronouns. Each page covers the literal meaning, who says it to whom, the mistake learners make, and four sentences you can use.

The four that depend on your own gender

오빠, 언니, 형 and 누나 are the terms that trip English speakers up most, because they encode the speaker as well as the listener. A woman addressing an older man says 오빠; a man in the identical position says 형. Learn the grid once and a whole class of errors disappears.

오빠oppaA woman's word for an older man she is close to. Never used by a male speaker. 언니eonniA woman's word for an older woman — and what you call the staff in a casual shop. 형hyeongA man's word for an older man. The moment a male friendship becomes real. 누나nunaA man's word for an older woman. Warm, but it names the age gap out loud.

The two that depend on when you arrived

선배 and 후배 have nothing to do with age or gender. They track who entered the school, club or company first — which means a younger 선배 is completely ordinary. One of the pair is an address term and the other is not.

선배seonbaeAnyone who joined before you. No gender, no age — just entry order. 후배hubaeA junior. The one word on this list you never say to the person's face.

The two suffixes that do the heavy lifting

Most everyday Korean address is a name with something attached. -씨 marks polite distance between equals; -님 raises the listener and goes on titles, names and usernames alike. Between them they cover the situations where no kinship term fits.

씨-ssiName plus -ssi: the polite default between adults who are not close. 님-nimThe honorific that attaches to titles, names and usernames alike.

The four borrowed from the family

Korean lends family vocabulary to people who are not family, which is why the woman running a restaurant is 이모 and the delivery man is 아저씨. Two of these are affectionate; two of them need care.

아저씨ajeossiA middle-aged man. Fine about someone, risky to them. 아줌마ajummaA middle-aged woman — the most sensitive word here. Use 저기요 instead. 이모imoMother's sister specifically, and the restaurant auntie by extension. 삼촌samchonFather's brother — and a lesson in how Korean counts kinship in degrees.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Which title should I learn first?
이름 + 씨 (name plus -ssi). It is the polite neutral setting between adults who are not close, it makes no assumption about age or gender, and it is never offensive. Everything else is a refinement on top of it.
Q. Do I really have to know someone's age?
In most contexts yes, which is why Koreans ask early — it is a practical question about which words to use rather than nosiness. Online is the exception: handle plus -님 works without knowing anything.
Q. Are these terms changing?
Slowly. Many companies have dropped rank titles in favour of name plus -님, and 아줌마 has lost ground as an address term. The kinship set — 오빠, 언니, 형, 누나 — is not going anywhere.
