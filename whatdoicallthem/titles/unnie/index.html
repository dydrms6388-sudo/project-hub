언니 eonni

언니 is the female-to-female half of the Korean kinship grid, and it is probably the most socially useful of the four terms, because it stretches beyond friendships into shops, salons and restaurants in a way the others do not.

Hangul언니
Revised Romanizationeonni
McCune–Reischauerŏnni
Literallya woman's older sister
Said byfemale speakers only
Said toan older female you are close to

What it actually means

언니 is a female speaker's elder sister, and like 오빠 it widens far past the family. An older classmate, a colleague you get along with, a friend's older sister, the woman two years above you in a dance class — all 언니, once the relationship is comfortable. Inside the family it is obligatory: calling your own older sister by her given name would sound cold or deliberately rude.

It also has a service-industry life of its own. Customers address staff in clothing shops, nail salons and small restaurants as 언니 when the woman looks roughly the same age or a little older, and staff use it back. In that register it is friendly shorthand rather than a claim of closeness, and it is far warmer than the alternatives.

Why you see two spellings

Revised Romanization — the system used on Korean road signs and in government documents — spells 언니 as eonni, because ㅓ is written eo. The spelling unnie that appears in subtitles, fan translations and K-pop coverage is an older transliteration that survived because it matches how English speakers hear the vowel. Neither is wrong for its purpose; write eonni in anything academic and expect unnie everywhere else.

The double n is not decorative. 언니 has a real geminate sound, and shortening it to a single n changes the rhythm of the word noticeably.

Who says it

Female speakers only. A male speaker addressing an older woman says 누나 (nuna). This is the same speaker-gender rule that governs 오빠 and 형, and it catches learners out in exactly the same way.

Unlike 오빠, 언니 carries no romantic charge at all, which is why it can be used so freely with strangers in service settings. The one place to slow down is at work, where a job title plus -님 outranks it and where jumping to 언니 with a senior colleague can sound presumptuous before you know each other.

The usual mistake — A male speaker using 언니, and — more commonly — a learner assuming eonni and unnie are two different words. They are one word with two romanization traditions.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Is it eonni or unnie?
Both spell 언니. eonni is Revised Romanization, the official system; unnie is the informal spelling that spread through subtitles and fan communities.
Q. Can I call a shop assistant unnie?
Yes, if she looks about your age or a little older and the setting is casual — clothing shops, nail salons, small restaurants. In a bank, a hospital or an office, use 선생님 or a job title instead.
Q. What does a man say instead?
누나 (nuna). The listener is the same; the speaker's gender changes the word.
