오빠 oppa

오빠 is the word English speakers meet first and misuse fastest. It literally names a woman's older brother, but in practice it covers almost any older male a woman is comfortable with, and it does something English titles never do: it encodes the speaker's own gender.

Hangul오빠
Revised Romanizationoppa
McCune–Reischaueroppa
Literallya woman's older brother
Said byfemale speakers only
Said toan older male you are close to

What it actually means

In its narrow sense 오빠 is a kinship term — a female speaker's elder brother. That narrow sense is still alive, and inside a family it is the only word used; a Korean woman almost never calls her older brother by his name. What makes the word confusing to learners is how far it travels outside the family. Once a woman is comfortable with an older man, 오빠 replaces his name entirely: at university, at a part-time job, in a hobby club, in a relationship. The word is not being used metaphorically in those cases. It is simply the correct address term for that slot.

Because it names a relationship rather than a person, 오빠 also works as a third-person reference. 오빠가 사 줬어 means "he bought it for me", where the "he" is understood from context to be the older male the speaker is close to. Learners who only drill the vocative use are surprised the first time they hear it in the middle of a sentence.

Who is allowed to say it

Only female speakers. This is the single hardest thing for English speakers to internalise, because English titles describe the listener and Korean kinship titles describe the pair. A man in exactly the same position — younger, close, addressing an older man — says 형 (hyeong) instead. There is no context in which a male speaker calling another man 오빠 sounds correct; at best it reads as a joke, at worst as a mistake that marks you as a drama viewer rather than a speaker.

The mirror image matters too. A woman addressing an older woman says 언니 (eonni), and a man addressing an older woman says 누나 (nuna). Four words, two speaker genders, two listener genders — memorise the grid once and a whole class of errors disappears.

The part nobody warns you about

오빠 carries warmth. That is a feature inside a family and a hazard outside one. Used with a man a woman has just met, it can read as flirtatious, and it is a running joke in Korea precisely because everyone knows it. The safe sequence is to start on 이름 + 씨 (given name plus -ssi), wait until he is unambiguously a friend, and let the switch to 오빠 happen naturally — often he will suggest it.

The same warmth is why 오빠 is the default address between a woman and her boyfriend when he is older, and why some couples keep using it after marriage. Nothing about that usage is childish, whatever English-language commentary sometimes claims; it is the ordinary register of Korean affection.

The usual mistake — A male speaker using 오빠, or a woman using it with a man she barely knows. The first is simply the wrong word; the second lands as flirting whether or not you meant it. Neither is offensive, but both are immediately noticeable.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Can a man say oppa?
No. A male speaker addressing an older male says 형 (hyeong). 오빠 encodes a female speaker, so a man using it reads as a mistake rather than as a stylistic choice.
Q. Is oppa always romantic?
No. Most uses are not romantic at all — brothers, older classmates, colleagues, friends of friends. It is warm rather than romantic, but that warmth is why using it too early with a stranger can be misread.
Q. How old does he have to be?
One year older is enough, because Korean seniority is counted by birth year. Someone born in the year before you counts as older even if the real gap is two months.
