Korean does not really have a word for “you”

Every learner hits the same wall in the first month. You know how to say hello, you know a handful of verbs, and then you meet an actual Korean person and freeze, because you have no idea what to put at the front of the sentence. English lets you say “hey, you” to almost anyone. Korean does not. The second-person pronouns that exist — 너 (neo) and 당신 (dangsin) — are either strictly for close friends and juniors or reserved for song lyrics, married couples and arguments. What fills the gap is a title: a kinship word, a school or workplace rank, a job position, or a name with a suffix welded onto it. Getting that one word right is most of what makes a beginner sound considerate rather than clumsy.

This calculator turns that decision into five questions. It needs your gender and theirs, because four of the most common titles — 오빠, 언니, 형, 누나 — encode the speaker's gender as well as the listener's. It needs both ages, because Korean seniority is counted by birth year and a single year is already a real gap. It needs the context, because the same two people can be 선배 and 후배 at a university club, 팀장님 and a name at work, and first names over dinner. And it needs the closeness level, because -님 and -씨 and a bare name mark three genuinely different distances. From those answers it returns one recommended title in Hangul with Revised Romanization, a short explanation of the rule that produced it, the alternatives that would also pass, three sentences you can copy, and the specific way that particular choice goes wrong.

The rule table is written by hand rather than machine-translated, and it deliberately encodes the awkward parts: that rank outranks age in an office, that 후배 is a word you use about someone and never to them, that calling a stranger 아줌마 can land as an insult, that in-law titles depend on whether the sibling belongs to your husband or your wife. Everything runs in your browser. There is no account, no name field and no server — the share link carries your answers inside the address itself.

How to use it

  1. Set the two gender chips. If neither fits you, pick the option you are read as in Korean-speaking settings — the gendered kinship terms follow perceived gender, and the calculator will fall back to gender-neutral options.
  2. Enter both ages. The gap line under the inputs tells you which side of the seniority line you are on before you even press the button.
  3. Choose the context. Work, school, friends, dating, in-laws, strangers and online communities all resolve differently, and “work” in particular ignores age almost entirely.
  4. Pick a closeness level. Move it up one notch whenever you are unsure — Korean forgives being too polite far more easily than the reverse.
  5. Read the “Watch out” box before you use the result. It is the part that stops the sentence sounding rude.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Can I call an older man oppa if I am a man?
No. 오빠 (oppa) is used only by female speakers for an older male; a male speaker says 형 (hyung). The same split runs the other way: 언니 (unnie) is female-to-female, 누나 (noona) is male-to-female. Because these four words encode the speaker's gender too, swapping them sounds wrong rather than merely unusual.
Q. How big does the age gap have to be before I stop using someone's name?
One year does it. Seniority is counted by birth year, not by birthday, so someone born the year before you is senior even if the actual gap is a few months. Same-birth-year people are 동갑 (dongap) and normally go straight to first names and casual speech.
Q. Which wins at work, age or job rank?
Rank, almost always. You address colleagues by position plus -님 — 팀장님, 과장님, 대리님 — whoever is older. Kinship-style terms like 형 or 언니 only appear once people are genuinely close, and rarely upward in the hierarchy unless the senior person offers first.
Q. Is it safe to call a stranger ajusshi or ajumma?
It is risky. Both words literally just mean a middle-aged man and a middle-aged woman, but 아줌마 in particular is often heard as a comment on age and manners. 저기요 (jeogiyo, “excuse me”) is the safe attention-getter, and 선생님 works as a polite form of address for an adult stranger even when they are not a teacher.
Q. Does this page store my answers or send them anywhere?
No. Everything runs in your browser, and the page never asks for a name, an email address or a phone number. Share links carry your answers encoded in the address after ?r= instead of on a server, so the person who opens the link rebuilds the same result locally.
