Why the second question is always “how old are you?”

A Korean conversation cannot proceed neutrally. Someone has to be older, and until that is settled neither speaker knows which verb endings or which titles to use.

The question is grammatical, not personal

English lets two strangers talk indefinitely without establishing anything about each other. Korean does not. The verb ending you choose, the address term you use and whether you may use someone's bare name all depend on the relationship between you, and age is the fastest available proxy. That is why 몇 년생이세요? — "what year were you born?" — arrives so early. It is closer to asking which language you would like to speak in than to asking a personal question.

Note the form of it. Koreans usually ask for the birth year rather than the age, because the year is what the system actually runs on.

Birth year, not birthday

Seniority is counted by birth year. Someone born in December of the year before you is your senior even though the real gap is a few weeks, and two people born eleven months apart across a January boundary are a full step apart in the hierarchy. People born in the same calendar year are 동갑 (dongap), and that is the one configuration with no senior and no junior — 동갑 pairs typically drop to casual speech and first names within minutes of discovering it.

The 빠른 년생 (fast-year) convention, where children born in January or February entered school a year early and socialised with the year above, muddied this for decades. It was phased out of the school system, but you will still meet people in their thirties whose social cohort does not match their birth year, and the polite move is to let them tell you which they use.

One year is a real gap

This is the part that surprises English speakers most. A single year is enough to make someone 형 or 언니 rather than a name, enough to change the verb endings, and enough that a Korean speaker will not casually drop it. To an English ear, treating a one-year difference as significant sounds absurd; in Korean, ignoring it is what sounds strange.

Where the hierarchy is not about age at all

Two large exceptions. In school, clubs and universities, the axis is entry year rather than birth year: whoever enrolled first is 선배, and a 선배 two years younger than you is unremarkable after military service or a repeated entrance exam. In a company, the axis is job rank, and it beats everything — a 32-year-old 팀장님 is addressed by that title by a 45-year-old on their team, without irony and without anyone finding it awkward.

When two axes collide, the institutional one wins in the institution and the age one wins outside it. The same two colleagues can be 팀장님 and 김지우 씨 at 4pm and 형 and 지우 at 9pm, and both are correct.

How to handle the question as a foreigner

Answer it. There is no advantage to deflecting, and the person asking needs the information to speak to you at all. If you would rather not, the graceful move is to name your birth year rather than your age, which is what most Koreans do anyway.

The reverse is also worth knowing: as a learner you are allowed to ask. 몇 년생이세요? is neutral and normal, and it is far better than guessing wrong and calling someone by a term that puts them a rung below where they belong.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Is it rude to ask a Korean person their age?
No, and it is expected in the other direction too. 몇 년생이세요? — asking the birth year — is the standard, slightly softer form.
Q. What happens if we are the same age?
You are 동갑 (dongap), the one relationship with no senior. Same-age pairs normally move to first names and casual speech very quickly.
Q. Does age beat job rank at work?
No. Rank wins inside the company, comprehensively. Age can reassert itself socially after hours, but never in a meeting.
Q. What is a 'fast year' birth?
빠른 년생 — people born in January or February who entered school a year early under the old system, and therefore socialise with the cohort above their birth year. The practice ended, but the people are still around.
Q. Do younger Koreans still care about this?
Less rigidly than a generation ago, and companies dropping rank titles has softened it further. But the grammar has not changed, so the question has not gone away.
