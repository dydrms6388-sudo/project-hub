Korean honorifics, explained without the grammar jargon

Korean marks respect in four places at once — the verb ending, the vocabulary, the particles and the form of address. Here is what each one does and which to learn first.

Respect is not one dial, it is four

English speakers usually picture politeness as a single slider: casual at one end, formal at the other. Korean splits the job across four independent systems, and a sentence can be polite in one and rude in another. The four are the speech level carried by the verb ending, honorific vocabulary that replaces ordinary words when the subject deserves elevation, particles that swap for honorific forms, and the address term you put at the front. Most beginner errors come from getting the ending right and the address term wrong — the calculator on this site exists for exactly that gap.

The three speech levels you need

Korean textbooks list six or seven speech levels. Three are in daily use. 합쇼체 (hapsyoche), ending in -습니다 / -ㅂ니다, is the formal-polite level: news broadcasts, presentations, the army, first meetings in business. 해요체 (haeyoche), ending in -아요 / -어요, is polite but relaxed, and it is the level a learner should default to for nearly everything — shops, taxis, classmates, colleagues. 반말 (banmal), the plain form with no ending attached, is casual speech for close friends of the same age and for people clearly younger.

The practical rule is that you may safely be one level too polite and never one level too casual. Start every relationship in 해요체 and let the other person invite you down. When they do, the phrase you will hear is 말 놓자 (mal notja) — "let's drop the formality".

Honorific vocabulary: different words, same meaning

Certain verbs and nouns have separate honorific forms used when the subject is someone you elevate. 먹다 (to eat) becomes 드시다; 자다 (to sleep) becomes 주무시다; 있다 (to be somewhere) becomes 계시다; 말 (words) becomes 말씀; 집 (house) becomes 댁; 이름 (name) becomes 성함; 나이 (age) becomes 연세. The honorific infix -시- does the same job for verbs with no special form: 가다 → 가시다.

The trap is that these elevate the subject of the sentence, not the listener. Saying 저는 드셨어요 about yourself is a real mistake — you have just honoured yourself. You eat 먹었어요; your grandmother 드셨어요.

-님 and -씨: the two suffixes

-씨 attaches to a given name and marks polite distance between adults who are broadly equal: 지우 씨, 민준 씨. It does not travel upward — using it with a boss or a professor sounds presumptuous. -님 raises the listener and attaches to almost anything: a role (선생님, 팀장님, 고객님), a name (지우 님), a username in a chat room. If you are unsure which to use with someone above you, -님 on their job title is the answer.

Particles change too

When the subject is honoured, the subject particle 이/가 becomes 께서 and the dative 에게 / 한테 becomes 께. 선생님께서 말씀하셨어요 stacks three of these systems in one short sentence: honorific particle, honorific verb, honorific address. You do not need to produce this in your first year, but recognising 께서 and 께 in speech tells you instantly who the speaker is elevating.

What to learn in what order

  1. 해요체 endings, until they are automatic.
  2. 이름 + 씨 and 직함 + 님 as your two default address forms.
  3. The kinship set — 오빠, 언니, 형, 누나 — and the speaker-gender rule behind them.
  4. The five or six most common honorific verbs: 드시다, 계시다, 주무시다, 말씀하시다, 돌아가시다.
  5. 합쇼체, once you need to present, interview or write formally.
  6. 반말, last — because knowing when you may use it matters more than knowing how.

Frequently asked questions

Q. What is the difference between polite and honorific?
Polite (해요체, 합쇼체) is about the listener and lives in the verb ending. Honorific (-시-, 드시다, 께서) is about the subject of the sentence, whoever that is. You can speak politely about yourself, but you cannot honour yourself.
Q. Will Koreans be offended if I use the wrong level?
Almost never with a visible learner — the effort registers before the error does. The risk is real once your Korean sounds fluent, because then the mistake looks like a choice.
Q. When can I switch to banmal?
When the other person offers, or when they are clearly younger and you are close. Asking 말 놓아도 될까요? is normal and polite; assuming it is not.
Q. Do I need 합쇼체 as a beginner?
Not to speak. Learn to recognise it — announcements, news and business email run on it — and produce 해요체 until you have a reason to need more formality.
Q. Does the honorific system apply to writing too?
Yes, and more strictly. Korean business email uses -님, 께, and 합쇼체 endings as a matter of course, and the address line at the top of a message is where a foreign writer is most likely to slip.
